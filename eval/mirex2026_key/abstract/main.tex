\title{K10: A Compact Convolutional Network for Global Key Estimation}
\oneauthor{John Hurliman}{Independent researcher, Oakland, CA, USA \\ {\tt jhurliman@jhurliman.org}}
\def\authorname{J. Hurliman}

\begin{abstract}
K10 is the MIREX 2026 Audio Key Detection submission from the open-source \texttt{jams}
project. It is a single 109k-parameter convolutional network that maps a whole-track
log-magnitude constant-Q transform to one of 24 major or minor keys in one CPU forward
pass, packaged as a script with bundled weights and a locked dependency set. The network
was trained only on the curated Beatport-preview annotations of mirdata's
\texttt{beatport\_key} corpus, with every selection made by cross-validation inside that
corpus. On the 567 usable GiantSteps Key excerpts, a single evaluation of the locked
model scored 0.832 weighted (symmetric fifth credit) and 0.780 exact, against 0.833 and
0.773 for madmom's default key processor on the same tracks; the paired difference
crosses zero under both fifth-credit conventions, so no ranking between the two is
claimed. GiantSteps Key had been used earlier in the project, which is disclosed in
full below and was confirmed as admissible by the task captains.
\end{abstract}

\section{System}
\label{sec:system}

\textbf{Input features.} Audio is decoded, downmixed to mono, and resampled to
22{,}050\,Hz. A constant-Q transform with 24 bins per octave is computed over 8 octaves
from C1 with hop 4{,}096 (about 5.4 frames per second) plus a 4-semitone margin on each
side (208 bins), and the margin is cropped after the transform, leaving 192 bins.
Magnitudes are compressed with $\log(1+x)$. The whole track is one input; nothing is
cropped or windowed at inference time.

\textbf{Architecture.} Table~\ref{tab:arch} lists the layers. Three blocks, each two
$3{\times}3$ convolutions with batch normalization and ELU followed by $2{\times}2$
max pooling, reduce the $192{\times}T$ input to $64$ channels at $24{\times}T/8$. After
dropout, average pooling over time only leaves a $64{\times}24$ map, which is flattened
into one linear layer with 24 output logits ordered tonic-major, tonic-minor for the
twelve tonics; the prediction is the argmax (no softmax is needed at inference). The
frequency positions are kept through to the readout by design: K10's dense readout uses
absolute pitch position, and an earlier variant with global pooling over both axes
failed in cross-validation. This is an implementation choice for this readout, not a
general claim about frequency pooling; the class-channel head of the all-convolutional
network in \cite{korzeniowski2018genre}, the family K10 belongs to, pools globally.
The model has 109{,}128 trainable parameters (450\,kB of weights) and no ensemble.

\textbf{Training.} Table~\ref{tab:train} gives the recipe of the run that produced the
submitted weights. Augmentation transposes each training example by an integer number of
semitones in $[-4, 4]$: the CQT is computed with the 4-semitone margin described above
and the 192-bin window is taken at a shifted offset within the padded representation,
with the label transposed to match (a shifted crop, not a circular roll). Architecture
width and the epoch budget were chosen by 5-fold cross-validation within the training
corpus, and the submitted weights are the model retrained on the full corpus at the
selected budget. The training script is \texttt{eval/train\_key\_cnn.py} in the
repository.

After cross-validation, three pre-registered candidates were compared by
cross-validation only, on the 1{,}129 training tracks shared with the project's older
GiantSteps-MTG feature set, using the out-of-fold CNN posteriors: the standalone CNN
(0.749), the existing two-head fusion with the CNN in its second slot (0.747), and the
fusion choosing between S-KEY and the CNN (0.749). The standalone CNN was selected as the
simplest within noise. The out-of-fold posteriors, the fusion inputs, and the run script
are committed with the training records, and \texttt{paper/key\_candidates\_cv.py}
recomputes the four numbers from them.

\section{Data and dataset-usage disclosure}
\label{sec:disclosure}

\textbf{Training corpus.} K10 was trained only on mirdata \texttt{beatport\_key}
\cite{faraldo2017thesis}: 1{,}486 Beatport preview excerpts whose key labels are the
curated annotations of the GiantSteps-MTG-Keys collection (revised by the dataset
authors, not Beatport's automatic metadata). A track is retained when its first listed annotation is exactly a tonic and
\emph{major} or \emph{minor} (flats mapped to sharps); the annotation confidence field
is not used. This keeps 1{,}363 tracks: 1{,}275 single-key annotations and 88 two-key
annotations (for example ``E minor $|$ E major''), for which the first listed key is
the label. The 123 dropped tracks are 115 single-key and 8 two-key annotations whose
first entry failed the major/minor parser; among the single-key ones, 75 are atonal
(``X''), 12 are ``other'', and 28 carry a modal qualifier (phrygian, dorian,
mixolydian, and similar). Audio was present for every track and none was dropped for
decoding. The retained set, its folds, and the drop list are reconstructed from
the public annotations and cross-checked against the run's out-of-fold record by
\texttt{paper/key\_train\_manifest.py}. The corpus has zero
track-identifier overlap with GiantSteps Key \cite{knees2015giantsteps}; the check
(\texttt{eval/verify\_key\_disjoint.py}) compares Beatport identifiers from the two
mirdata indexes (1{,}486 versus 600, empty intersection). Identifier disjointness does
not rule out the same recording appearing under two identifiers. No Isophonics or other
key data was used.

\textbf{Evaluation subset.} The development evaluation uses the 567 GiantSteps Key
excerpts (of 604 annotated; 600 with retrievable audio) whose public annotation the
acquisition script could reduce to one major or minor reference. Many of these are not
plain single-key labels: the acquisition script (\texttt{eval/acquire\_dataset.py}) maps flats to sharps and
reduces each label to one major or minor key by mode family (aeolian, dorian, phrygian,
and ``harmonic'' to minor; ionian, mixolydian, and lydian to major). Two-key annotations
such as ``C major $|$ C minor phrygian'' are collapsed by the same rule applied to the
whole string: the tonic of the first key, and minor if any minor-family word appears.
Of the 567 references, 378 are plain major/minor (including ionian and aeolian), 98 are
single keys with another modal qualifier, and 91 are collapsed two-key annotations, of
which 15 match neither listed key (``C major $|$ A minor'' became C minor). The 33
excluded excerpts carry ``other'', ``X'', or malformed labels. Both systems were scored
against the same references; incorrect references can still favor one system's
predictions over the other's, and the subset analyses in Section~\ref{sec:results}
likewise show no statistically detectable difference.

\textbf{Prior use of GiantSteps Key in this project.} The MIREX 2026 rules ask
participants to account for any development use of the evaluation data. The account
below is taken from the project's public experiment ledger
(\url{https://github.com/jhurliman/jams/blob/main/paper/EXPERIMENTS.md}), which records every evaluation
with its date and commit.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Before K10 existed, the project evaluated and error-analysed several key systems
on the same 567 excerpts: an Essentia template matcher \cite{faraldo2016key,
bogdanov2013essentia}, S-KEY \cite{kong2025skey}, madmom's key CNN
\cite{korzeniowski2018genre,bock2016madmom}, and two small logistic refinements of the
template output fit on GiantSteps-MTG-Keys.
\item One retired predecessor, a mode classifier, had been fit on GiantSteps Key itself.
It was identified as contaminated and replaced before any comparison was reported.
\item The error analysis of those banked predictions motivated training a stronger base
model. That decision is the extent of the benchmark's influence on K10: its
architecture, hyperparameters, and weights were selected by cross-validation within
\texttt{beatport\_key}, under a pre-registered decision rule recorded in the ledger
before training.
\item The locked K10 model was evaluated exactly once on the 567 excerpts. The
descriptive breakdowns in Section~\ref{sec:results} were computed afterwards from that
one set of predictions; no training or tuning followed. The submitted weights are
byte-identical to the evaluated checkpoint\newline (MD5 {\footnotesize\texttt{6141daf25376c16a7bc4326b742e4a3c}}).
\item This history was sent to the task captains before submission; they confirmed on
28 September 2026 that K10 is admissible for the ranked results with this disclosure.
\end{itemize}
Published GiantSteps Key scores (for example madmom's 0.746 in
\cite{korzeniowski2018genre}) were obtained on different track sets and model
configurations and are not matched comparisons with the numbers below.

\textbf{Reference-label sensitivity.} Rescoring the same predictions (symmetric
fifth) after excluding only the 15 references that match neither listed key gives K10
0.849 and madmom 0.848 (paired $+0.001$ \ci{-0.018}{+0.020}); on the 476 references
that come from a single annotated key, 0.867 and 0.869 (exact 0.826 for both; paired
$-0.002$ \ci{-0.022}{+0.018}); and on the 378 plain major/minor references, 0.870 and
0.877 (paired $-0.007$ \ci{-0.029}{+0.015}). Each restriction also changes the
composition of the evaluation set, so the higher subset scores do not isolate the
effect of label normalization; what the three subsets show is that none of them
establishes an advantage for either system.

These are development-time results on a subset the project had used before, so they
give an expectation rather than a prediction of the MIREX score, which is computed on
the full 604-excerpt set with the organizers' scorer and references. No listening audit was performed, so the errors have not been related to
musical causes; per-genre means (with counts) are in the output of
\texttt{paper/key\_scores.py} and are descriptive only.

\section{Submission package and measurements}
\label{sec:package}

The package contains \texttt{predict\_key.py} (calling contract
\texttt{predict\_key.py \%input \%output}; one tab-separated line, tonic then mode,
sharps-only tonics), whose PEP~723 inline metadata declares \texttt{torch} 2.8,
\texttt{librosa} 0.10 or later below 1.0, \texttt{numpy} 1.26 to 2.2, and Python
3.10--3.12; the lockfile \texttt{predict\_key.py.lock} that fixes the tested
environment (torch 2.8.0, librosa 0.11.0, numpy 2.2.6, soundfile 0.14.0;
\texttt{uv run --locked} installs exactly that set); the weights
\texttt{key\_cnn\_v1.pt}; a README; and this abstract. Inference uses the same CQT configuration as training. The
training pipeline cached features as float16 before casting back to float32, whereas
the package keeps float32; on the 567 development excerpts the two paths agree on every
argmax, with a maximum absolute softmax difference of $7.5\times10^{-5}$.

Table~\ref{tab:runtime} gives end-to-end measurements of the packaged command on one
120-second 44.1\,kHz 16-bit mono WAV, including interpreter start and model load, with
\texttt{torch} limited to one thread. The Linux install resolved the default CUDA build
of torch, which raises install size and resident memory; the GPU is never used. Memory
scales with track length through the full-track CQT.
