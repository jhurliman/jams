\begin{abstract}
\jams{} is an open-source analysis service for electronic dance music: key, tempo,
beats, functional structure, stems, and per-stem MIDI from one HTTP call, with every
reported number regenerated by committed code. Rebuilding standard benchmarks under a
single paired-statistics protocol surfaced a calibration problem that affects how key
detection results are compared. The strongest published GiantSteps~Key system gains
$+0.087$ weighted score when re-run on our 567-track usable subset, six times the
field's total claimed progress since 2018, so subset-measured scores cannot be compared
against published full-set scores at all. Under same-subset paired tests, a
109k-parameter CNN trained on public Beatport annotations ties that system
($\Delta{=}-0.001$, 95\% CI $[-0.019, +0.018]$) with better exact accuracy and
MIT-licensed weights. We demonstrate the service and its companion web application
(waveform, beat grid, segment editor, per-stem piano rolls) running locally on a
laptop.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

DJ and production workflows ask a small set of questions about any track: its key, its
tempo, where the beats and phrase boundaries fall, and what the constituent parts are.
EDM is the genre family where these questions matter most operationally and where
general-purpose MIR models underperform, since half-time drum\,\&\,bass, modally
ambiguous pads, and drops that behave nothing like pop choruses sit in the tails of
most training distributions.

\jams{} packages an EDM-focused answer as a local service (macOS and Linux) exposing a
single endpoint.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/jhurliman/jams} (MIT). The
repository contains the service, acquisition and scoring scripts for every dataset
used, an experiments ledger that every claim traces to, and the statistics script that
regenerates every confidence interval reported here.} Analysis capabilities and their
held-out results: key (0.832 MIREX weighted, \S\ref{sec:calibration}), tempo (0.967
Acc1 on corrected labels, $n{=}458$), beats and structure via an on-device All-In-One
ensemble \cite{kim2023allinone,kim2026raveform} (0.978 beat-F), SCNet
separation \cite{tong2024scnet}, and a mix-to-MIDI cascade whose accompaniment
transcription (0.788 note-F on Slakh2100 \cite{manilow2019slakh}) exceeds the
\emph{ground-truth-stem} ceiling of the widely deployed basic-pitch transcriber
(0.490) \cite{bittner2022basicpitch,chang2024yourmt3}. Everything learned that ships
is MIT-licensed, trained by us on public data.

This abstract focuses on the finding we most want the community to poke at before the
full paper: benchmark-subset calibration in key detection.

\section{The Subset-Calibration Finding}\label{sec:calibration}

GiantSteps~Key \cite{knees2015giantsteps} ships 604 annotations. In our pull, 600 have
retrievable preview audio; like prior work we drop atonal, multi-key, and unparseable
labels, leaving $n{=}567$. Such subsets are routine and rarely identical across
papers. To measure what subset selection alone does to scores, we re-ran the strongest
published system, the madmom key CNN \cite{korzeniowski2018genre}, on our subset with
its published weights and default settings. Published full-set score: 0.746 weighted.
On our subset: \textbf{0.833}, a $+0.087$ shift from track selection alone
(\figref{fig:forest}).

For scale, total claimed progress across published GiantSteps~Key systems from 2018 to
2026 (0.746 to 0.759 \cite{keymyna2026}) is $0.013$. The subset effect is more than
six times larger than eight years of reported improvement. Any comparison of a
subset-measured number with a published full-set number is therefore uninterpretable,
including comparisons that favor our own systems. The only rankings we trust are
same-subset paired tests, and we publish the subset track list and per-track outputs
so future systems can be compared on exactly these terms.

\section{Parity Without the License Restriction}\label{sec:parity}

Under that protocol we evaluated a deliberately small key CNN of our own: log-CQT
input, three convolutional blocks, time-only average pooling so frequency structure
reaches the readout, and a 24-way softmax; 109k parameters, 0.45\,MB. It trains in two
hours on \texttt{beatport\_key}, a revised-annotation superset of GiantSteps-MTG-Keys
\cite{faraldo2017thesis} (1{,}363 usable tracks, zero track-id overlap with the test
set). The experiment was pre-registered in the public ledger with a fixed decision
rule; all model selection used training-corpus cross-validation, and the locked model
received exactly one test evaluation.

The result (\tabref{tab:key}) is a statistical dead heat with the strongest published
system: paired $\Delta{=}-0.001$ \ci{-0.019}{+0.018} against the madmom CNN, with 486
of 567 tracks scored identically, alongside the best exact accuracy measured (0.780
vs.\ 0.773). Under the pre-registered rule this is a failure to establish superiority.
What it establishes instead is parity from a model anyone can train on public data,
with weights that carry no non-commercial restriction; the madmom weights are
CC~BY-NC-SA, which \jams{} uses for evaluation only and does not ship.

\section{Findings That Travel}\label{sec:findings}

The same harness produced measurement results that changed our numbers by more than
typical model improvements do. Slakh bass MIDI follows written convention: scoring
sounding-pitch output against it without the $+12$ semitone correction turns 0.80
note-F into 0.04. The 16\,kHz preview build of Slakh caps drum onset-F near 0.46
versus 0.64 at full bandwidth. GiantSteps tempo labels are wrong for half-time genres;
correcting them from the v2 re-annotation \cite{schreiber2018crowd} moves our tempo
stack from 0.919 to 0.967 Acc1, a larger effect than any model change we measured. In
separator selection, SCNet's $+2.7$\,dB SI-SDR gain on drums over HT-Demucs
\emph{costs} one point of downstream drum onset-F, so we score separators on the task
they feed. And three pre-registered variants of class-rebalanced structure training
all failed their gates, every per-class gain funded by an adjacent-class regression;
the negative result ships in the ledger with the same statistics as the positives.

\section{Demo}\label{sec:demo}

The demo is the full stack live on a laptop: drop in a track, get key, tempo, beat and
downbeat grid, functional segments (intro/buildup/drop/breakdown), stems, and per-stem
MIDI in one response, then inspect everything in the companion web application, which
overlays the beat grid and segment boundaries on the waveform and renders each stem's
transcription as a piano roll. The same interface is the label-inspection tool behind
our error analyses, so visitors can see exactly how the reported numbers were audited,
per track, against the ledger.
